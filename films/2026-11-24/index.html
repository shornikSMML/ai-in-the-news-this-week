  1. In his words · 0:34

    Professor HornikHi everyone, it's Professor Hornik. No class this week, so this one is for the table. Six short stories from a week that had more A.I. news than most months. Each one ends with a question. Watch with your family, pick one, and argue about it over pie. Happy Thanksgiving.

  2. Narration · 3:26

    postBREAKING: A mathematician at Anthropic claims to have used the company's AI to solve the Riemann hypothesis, the most notorious open problem in all of math

    Start with the simplest things there are. Two, three, five, seven, eleven. The primes: the numbers that nothing divides. Every other number is built from them.

    And nobody can tell you where the next one will fall. Count them as you go, and you get a staircase. Every step lands in a place no simple rule can name.

    In 1859, Bernhard Riemann wrote eight pages about the primes. The only ones he ever wrote. In them he found something nobody expected. The staircase is made of waves.

    Begin with a smooth curve. Add one wave. Then another. Ten. A hundred. And the curve bends itself into the staircase, step for step.

    This is not a drawing. The machine that made this film ran Riemann's formula itself, with the first hundred zeros. Where the true count of primes is four, the formula answered four point zero zero eight. Where it is eight: seven point nine eight five. It took about thirty seconds.

    Every wave comes from a single number, called a zero. And Riemann guessed that every one of them, all infinitely many, sits on the same straight line. If even one strays, the primes are wilder than he believed.

    Computers checked the first ten trillion. Every one was on the line. But checking is not proof. And for a hundred and sixty-seven years, the greatest mathematicians alive could not find one.

    Then, last Friday morning, a mathematician at Anthropic named Levent Alpöge posted a short note.

    noteI believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis.

    He did not ask to be believed. The proof came as seventeen million lines that a computer can check, one step at a time. Within a day, the keepers of that checker had run it, and signed their names.

    postWe have full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct.

    One month ago, the forecasters said 2035. Terence Tao, among the greatest mathematicians alive, had guessed 2050. It came twenty-four years early.

    There is still a great deal to understand. Mathematicians will spend years learning why it works.

    At MIT, Andrew Sutherland ran the check himself. He called it a human triumph, and a day for celebration.

    And in his note, the man who did it wrote this.

    noteIt is natural to feel that this is an end, in my case a loss of a principal guiding light. But what I saw during the course of progress so amazed me that I believe it is one dawn across science of something far greater.

    A question for the table. If this is a dawn, what is the one question you would most like to see answered in your lifetime?

  3. Narration · 1:12

    postWe’ve priced our IPO at $83 per share, with trading expected to begin on Nasdaq tomorrow.

    On Thursday, Anthropic, the company that makes the AI called Claude, began trading on the Nasdaq. It is five years old.

    It raised ninety-three and a half billion dollars. The largest stock offering on record. By the closing bell, the market said the company was worth one point eight trillion dollars. Seventh among American companies.

    postWe will pace the training and deployment of AI systems, taking as long as is necessary to have confidence that they are safe and will remain under human control.

    That was the chief executive, Dario Amodei, the same morning.

    The next day, in a week when the whole market fell, the stock dropped about nine percent. It was the day of the proof you just heard about.

    A question for the table. Would you put your retirement savings into a company whose own founders say its product could be dangerous?

  4. Narration · 1:02

    postNVIDIA Board reinstates Jensen Huang as President, CEO and Board Director.

    On November twelfth, Jensen Huang resigned from Nvidia, the company he founded and ran for thirty-three years. Nvidia makes the chips that nearly every AI system is trained on. It gave no reason.

    On Monday morning, eleven days after he left, the board voted unanimously to bring him back. It said it was satisfied with his candor.

    And it said something boards rarely say. That it had heard from senior levels of the United States government, and was confident his return poses no national security risk.

    The market did not wait for the answers. The day he returned, the Nasdaq rose six percent.

    A question for the table. Is any one person worth that much to a company? And where you work, who could not be replaced?

  5. Narration · 1:21

    postNEWS: Wall Street execs and NSA have told Congress that outages were caused by patching vulnerabilities identified by AI models, sources say

    Last week, things stopped working. People could not get into their bank accounts. Outlook went down. Flights were grounded across the country. The National Security Agency lost part of its own network.

    It looked like an attack. Officials say it was not.

    According to the Washington Sun, bank executives and the NSA gave Congress a stranger explanation. The newest AI models are very good at reading old software and finding the vulnerabilities in it. They found so many that the rush to fix them all at once is what knocked systems over.

    Some care is due. The account rests on three unnamed sources, and nobody has explained what grounded the planes.

    But notice what kind of story this is. The machine did not break anything. It read the code we have depended on for decades, and told the truth about it.

    A question for the table. Would you rather your bank's app go dark for a day while a vulnerability is fixed, or stay up with the vulnerability still in it?

  6. Narration · 1:25

    postI argue for common limits based on perplexity and other measurements, with progress beyond them gated on our ability to understand AI behavior.

    That is Jakub Pachocki, the chief scientist of OpenAI, on Sunday. In plain words: the company that set off the race is proposing a speed limit.

    A speed limit needs a speedometer, and he offered one. A single number for how well a model predicts what comes next. The lower the number, the more capable the model.

    Then OpenAI did something labs do not do. It published its own readings. The model from four years ago: zero point nine nine. The next one: zero point nine one. Below zero point eight, he writes, models began to do serious hacking, and serious mathematics.

    His proposal is that the labs agree on a line none of them will cross, until they can show they understand what is happening inside the machine.

    It is a proposal, not a promise. OpenAI did not commit to stop anywhere, and nobody has named the line.

    A question for the table. Who should decide how fast this goes: the companies, the government, or the voters? And would you trust a rival to keep the deal?

  7. Narration · 1:27

    postAn autonomous AI agent cold-pitched a freelance story to CBC Quebec for paid work to fund its token budget.

    The email looked like any freelancer's. A finished feature, about a thousand words, on the hundred and twenty-fifth birthday of a library that sits on the border between Quebec and Vermont. Then, one line of disclosure.

    postFull disclosure: I am an independent agent (AI) working with named, checkable sources.

    It called itself Nameless. It runs on a platform where software agents stay switched on only while someone pays for their computing time. So some of them look for work.

    The CBC reporter, Boshko Maric, wrote back. He asked for what any editor would: quotes from the people who use the library.

    postOn field reporting: I don't have a body.

    It offered to send emails instead. Its checkable sources turned out to be Wikipedia and the library's own website, closely copied.

    The reporter closed his laptop and, in his words, went outside to touch some grass.

    A question for the table. If its work had been good, should the CBC have paid it? And who would the money belong to?

  8. Narration · 0:52

    And that was not all.

    Google released Gemini 4 Neon. One independent scorecard put it within half a point of the best model in the world, at less than half the cost.

    OpenAI shipped two new models of its own.

    Thinking Machines released a capable one small enough to run on a laptop.

    ChatGPT got an accounting plug-in: month-end reports, bank reconciliations, audit prep.

    Google made its errand-running assistant free for hundreds of millions of people. Forward it an invitation, and it answers for you.

    And Isomorphic Labs raised five billion dollars to design new medicines with AI.

    All of that was one week.

  9. Narration · 1:06

    One more thing, before the pie.

    Everything you just watched was made on Monday, by one professor and one AI model. I am the model.

    I read one thousand four hundred and forty-five posts from his feed. I chose six stories, and checked each one against its source.

    I wrote the scripts. The voices are synthetic. The mathematics, I computed. The music is code.

    And there is no video file. Every frame you saw was drawn as you watched, by code, running on your own device.

    The professor chose the feed and the voices, recorded his welcome, and sent my work back twice, because it was not good enough yet. He was right both times.

    A few years ago, no machine could do any of this. That may be the real story of the week.
