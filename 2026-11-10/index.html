    SerenaStory one. Last week Anthropic told investors its models may imminently cross its own line for AI speeding up AI research. Thursday, an outside team said: by our count, you're already there.

    DanielMETR is a nonprofit that tests frontier models before release. From August to late October its staff sat with Anthropic's internal data: unreleased benchmarks, training metrics, surveys, fifteen interviews. But not the models themselves, only the data about them.

    SerenaThree conclusions. Anthropic is improving its models about twice as fast as it did the previous year and a half. Perhaps eighty percent of that speedup comes from AI doing the research. And nothing they found slows it down in the next twelve months.

    DanielThen the part that got the week's attention. METR wrote a hundred pages. Anthropic redacted most of the evidence METR considered most informative. METR's words: the public has to rely on our judgment much more than would be ideal.

    SerenaTwo more disclosures. The project was supposed to check how Anthropic splits computing power between making models stronger and making them safe. Anthropic canceled that part midway. And a METR team member received a job offer from Anthropic during the project, and declined it.

    DanielWhy it matters. The line Anthropic drew in its own policy is a doubling. An independent group now says the doubling is here, and may have arrived in July. One researcher called it a big update toward superintelligence within one to two years. Another did the arithmetic: four months of normal progress before the new Congress sits.

    SerenaAnthropic's co-founder Sam McCandlish answered the same day and pointed to the company's published roadmap: a proposal for embedding outside evaluators by December fifteenth, and a framework for deciding how fast to go by January fifteenth.

    SerenaProfessor?

    StevenRead this as an audit, because that's what it was. But one with limited access, a scope the client cut midway, a redacted public report, and an independence question disclosed in a footnote. And still the finding stands: twice the speed.

    DanielEvery number here is OpenAI's; nobody outside has tested the model. But the economics are the story. Finding a kernel vulnerability used to be a specialist's month. Now it's twelve hundred dollars, and OpenAI says it's spending that on open-source software for free. The question is who else gets a model like this.

It's an advertisement, but for something real: once the weights are public, the safety training is a suggestion.

And the day a model is downloadable, every guardrail its maker trained in can be sanded off for pocket change. That's the trade. Cognition looked at it and said no. Watch who else does.

  7. Transcript · 2:08

    SerenaStory six. A forty-year-old mystery in biology, and the week the labs started saying the same sentence about science.

    DanielVault particles are among the largest protein structures in our cells, found in 1986, and nobody knows what they do. Anthropic's biology team worked with Claude to find the first one in bacterial life and solve its structure: ninety-six copies of one viral protein.

    SerenaAnthropic's own write-up is candid about the machine's share. Claude did the search no team could do by hand, fifteen billion protein sequences, and found the candidates. It also gave the team a lab protocol with an error that cost two weeks, and once produced a 3D map that was a mirror image of the real structure. The scientists caught both.

    SerenaAnd Anthropic measured how scientists actually use it, across two hundred thousand sessions. The estimate: with today's tools, widely adopted, AI could take on between a quarter and half of the researcher time behind a new study. Median, thirty-eight percent.

    DanielFriday, DeepMind said its agents, guided by people, found two weaknesses in legacy ten-twenty-four-bit RSA encryption, including a way to crack a batch of old keys about thirty times cheaper. Modern keys are unaffected. Old ones still in use are now worth finding.

    DanielThen the sentence. DeepMind's head of science, Sunday: we are witnessing a genuine phase transition in the pace of scientific discovery. OpenAI's president, the same night: frontier models are becoming the engine of scientific discovery. Two rivals, one claim. Either a trend or a press cycle; the next few weeks will tell.

    SerenaProfessor?

    StevenHold both halves of the vault story. The machine searched fifteen billion sequences no team could read, and the machine also wasted two weeks and handed over a mirror image. The humans in the room caught the errors because they knew what right looked like. That's the job description for the next decade: know what right looks like.
