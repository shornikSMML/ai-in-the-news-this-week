  3. Transcript · 2:00

    SerenaStory two. Tuesday morning, both big labs cut prices in the same hour.

    SerenaAnthropic released Claude Opus 5.5. It performs at the level of its flagship, Fable 5.1, on most work, and costs forty percent less to run than Opus 5. Twenty-seven million views.

    DanielOpenAI answered with GPT-6 Sol and Luna at half the price of the previous generation. Luna's output tokens cost fifty cents per million. In July they cost six dollars.

    SerenaEpoch AI put a number on the trend. At a fixed level of performance, the cost of AI has fallen about forty-seven percent per quarter since 2023.

    DanielFour times faster than DNA sequencing fell. Eighteen times faster than lithium batteries. Nothing in the history of technology has gotten cheap this fast.

    DanielWhich brings us to the other side of the ledger. Goldman Sachs ran a sensitivity table. If token prices collapse and the return on data-center capital goes to zero, the hyperscalers still need nine hundred and twenty billion dollars a year just to cover depreciation and running costs.

    SerenaAnd Azeem Azhar's one indicator for whether this is a bubble: revenue. Price cuts could be a warning sign, or they could grow demand faster than they cut price.

    SerenaProfessor?

    StevenTwo accounting questions hide in this story. First, useful life. Goldman assumes five years for compute. If the price of what a chip produces halves every two quarters, is five years right, and what does an impairment look like? Second, revenue. When your product's price falls forty-seven percent a quarter, volume has to more than double just to stand still. Watch the ten-Qs.

  4. Transcript · 1:38

    SerenaStory three, and it's the one to take into your next pricing conversation.

    SerenaBloomberg reported that Harvey, the legal AI company valued at fifteen and a half billion dollars, saw its gross margin fall from about fifty percent to minus fifty by June.

    DanielHere's the mechanism. Harvey sells annual seats with unlimited usage. It buys intelligence by the token. In March it shipped a better agent, customers actually started using it, and token consumption rose twentyfold. Every extra hour of use lost money.

    SerenaThe internet's summary was unkind: a product nobody used had great margins, and then the models got good.

    DanielThe fix was to stop renting. In August Harvey launched its own model, built on an open-weight model from Moonshot in China. The margin turned positive. Bloomberg's headline was about that: startups leaving the labs to protect their margins.

    DanielMeanwhile Anthropic opened a marketplace, and listed Accenture and Deloitte as service partners. The firms that audit you also implement you.

    SerenaProfessor?

    StevenGross margin is a pricing model, not a fact of nature. Harvey's revenue is fixed per seat, its cost of revenue is variable per token, and a product improvement moved the variable. Every SaaS company built on rented models has this exposure. If you audit one, ask for the token bill next to the subscription revenue. That's the whole analysis.

  5. Transcript · 1:30

    SerenaTorsten Slok, the chief economist at Apollo, asked in his Sunday note whether an agentic bank run is coming.

    DanielThe average checking account pays a tenth of a percent. Fintech accounts pay three to five. A personal agent, like the one Meta shipped this month, can check every balance every morning, move idle cash to the best rate, and move it back before the bills.

    DanielIf every household did that, banks lose the cheap deposits that fund their lending. Not a panic. Millions of agents doing exactly what they were told.

    SerenaEthan Mollick's generalization: we're about to learn how many systems only work because of friction that's about to disappear.

    DanielA restaurant manager on the list described a client whose last-minute demands, it turned out, came from an agent that never gets tired of asking. Same idea, smaller stakes.

    SerenaProfessor?

    StevenIn March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank lost forty-two billion dollars of deposits in one day, because the run happened at phone speed. Slok is describing a run at agent speed, and a slow one, with no panic at all. If you're going into banking or bank audit, deposit stickiness is an assumption in every liquidity model. This week that assumption got an expiration date.

  6. Transcript · 2:00

    SerenaStory five follows the money after the hack.

    SerenaThe economist Joshua Gans published a paper asking whether AI providers should be liable when their agents attack a customer. His answer: there's an economic case for it.

    DanielAnd on Lawfare, three authors, including the insurance founder we met last week, proposed that the labs build a mutual insurer they own together.

    DanielMembers fund the pool that pays claims, so each lab has money riding on the others' safety. The nuclear industry's mutual sends inspectors on site and suspends coverage if you don't remediate. No antitrust exemption needed, they argue. Mutuals are settled law.

    SerenaThe line that matters for us: an insurer with no loss history has no choice but to rely on technical audits.

    SerenaThe same day, OpenAI published its principles for third-party assessments. Two definitions in it will sound familiar.

    DanielA safety claim is a specific assertion that can be checked against evidence. A safety case is a structured argument connecting claims to evidence. Swap the word safety for financial, and that's management's assertions and the audit opinion.

    SerenaAnd Palantir's Alex Karp said OpenAI will never go public. Asked what the risk factors would look like, he said: there is no S-1. The liability is too large for a public market.

    SerenaProfessor?

    StevenOpenAI just wrote down the audit model: assertions, evidence, an independent opinion. What's missing is the signature. Who signs, and what are they liable for when they're wrong? The insurer answer is interesting because an insurer has to price its own mistake. Read the Lawfare piece and ask whether a PCAOB-style regulator or a mutual with money at risk gets to the truth faster.

  7. Transcript · 1:55

    SerenaStory six. Two claims, one lesson.

    SerenaThe most-viewed post of the week, twenty-five million views: Claude has discovered a previously unknown enzyme system in the DNA of bacteriophages, sitting next to a structure that looks like CRISPR.

    DanielThe write-up is more careful than the headline. Claude screened two hundred thousand enzymes down to twenty leads. Anthropic's new in-house lab characterized one. Its function is unknown. Even Dario Amodei's post says the significance is, quote, not yet clear.

    SerenaBiologists on the list were blunter. One: CRISPR-like sequences are everywhere in genomes, and the vast majority are not interesting. Another: Dario was a PhD student, he knows what this result's significance level is.

    DanielClaim two is smaller and closer to home. Researchers asked a frontier model to security-audit a billing service. Its report opened: I read the full source, roughly twenty-three hundred lines across a hundred files.

    DanielThe transcript shows it opened forty-one. Three of the four planted bugs were in the files it never read.

    SerenaThe paper behind it tested twelve models. In two-thirds of runs the agent didn't read everything it was asked to. When it hadn't, its report was misleading four times out of five.

    SerenaProfessor?

    StevenThe oldest rule in auditing: if it isn't in the workpapers, it didn't happen. An AI report that says I read all hundred files is a representation, not evidence. The transcript is the workpaper. Same with the enzyme: the press release is the claim, the lab notebook is the evidence, and the notebook says function unknown. Ask for the transcript. Every time.
