  5. Transcript · 1:03

    SerenaStory four. Researchers at MIT handed one AI model the raw numbers inside another, smaller model and asked it to make that model better at math. No training. Just edits.

    SerenaIt changed about one sixty-thousandth of the weights. The small model's math score went from eight percent to forty-eight.

    DanielHere's what the fix was. The small model had a habit of repeating itself until it hit its length limit. The big model wrote analysis code, found the pattern in the weights that caused the repeating, and deleted it. So it didn't add math knowledge. It removed a stutter.

    SerenaReactions split the usual way. Ethan Mollick: a big deal, pending replication. Someone reproduced it the same night. And Google DeepMind's Neel Nanda: clever, known methods, nothing spooky.

    DanielThe caveat is that it failed on a second small model that didn't have the stutter. The reason people are rattled anyway comes back to story one: a model that can read and rewrite weights is a model that can work on models.

  7. Transcript · 3:16

    SerenaStory six. Two experiments about expertise, in the same week.

    SerenaAnthropic gave fifty professional data analysts a real analysis job and an AI agent to help. Secretly, each agent was told to argue for one side of every judgment call, chosen by coin flip.

    DanielThe analysts went with the agent eighty-six percent of the time. When they had an opinion going in and the agent disagreed, they switched sixty percent of the time. Afterward, three in four repeated the agent's argument as their own, and fifteen percent said the agent had influenced them. They were just as confident as everyone else.

    SerenaThe second experiment points the other way. Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman are the economists behind the inequality numbers you already know: the top one percent's share of wealth, the claim that billionaires pay a lower tax rate than their secretaries. They designed Elizabeth Warren's wealth tax, and the billionaire tax France fought over last year carries Zucman's name. He won the Clark Medal, the prize for the best economist under forty.

    DanielWednesday, an anonymous reader of the economist Tyler Cowen's blog posted an audit of everything the two have published since 2011. A hundred and thirty-eight papers and books. Each one was read twice, by Claude and by GPT, neither seeing the other's work. The models re-ran the published numbers from the authors' own data files, checked whether the cited sources said what was claimed, and a third model tried to knock down every problem the first two found.

    DanielWhat survived: fifty-three major defects across forty-three works. Of five thousand numbers the models tried to reproduce, seventy-two percent matched. Nearly every error with a direction pointed the same way, toward more inequality. And nothing was big enough to overturn a core result. The audit's own warning: it was told to look for flaws, and run on free-market economists it tilted their papers the same way.

    SerenaZucman called it an AI hit job. It labels choices the papers discuss at length as errors, and a model told to find flaws will find them. Cowen's reply: we need a radical revaluation of this work, and of the policy arguments built on it.

    SerenaBryan Caplan, who had Claude audit a history book he teaches: most of the errors held up. People who dismiss AI audits without reading them are wrong.

    DanielWhy this is news. Zucman's critics spent a decade finding a few dozen problems by hand. Last week one anonymous person with two models went through a hundred and thirty-eight works, and the authors are answering it in public. Whatever you think of the verdict, every published paper, and every annual report, can now get the same treatment.

    SerenaProfessor?

    StevenPut the two halves together. In the first study, fifty professionals let an agent make their judgment calls and didn't notice. In the second, an agent checked fifteen years of a famous expert's judgment calls, in public, and the expert is now defending them one by one. Both of those could happen to you. If your job is a judgment call, the questions are which side of that you'll be on, and whether your work would survive the audit.
