  3. Transcript · 2:07

    SerenaStory three. Who audits the labs, and who writes the rules. This week Anthropic did both.

    SerenaPolitico obtained a draft bill Anthropic is circulating in Congress. The reaction that went viral: under this bill, the company could kill forty-nine people and cause up to a billion dollars of damage before anyone could legally stop it.

    DanielThe draft's thresholds: intervention only for an imminent risk above a billion dollars or fifty deaths, in three categories. Independent evaluators who register with Commerce and can't be paid on outcomes. And one agency, Commerce, allowed to ask a judge to pause a model.

    SerenaThe policy crowd gave partial credit. Charlie Bullock: more than other labs have done, but weaker than the bipartisan bill already in the House, and, his words, kind of wild that there's no government licensing for the independent auditors. Nathan Calvin: viscerally angry.

    DanielThe day before, Anthropic published how its own embedded evaluators work. Six people from METR now have employee-like access, on company hardware.

    DanielThree tiers. At the top, evaluators publish without approval. Anthropic can't veto, but it can require redactions in categories it defines, and the evaluator can say whether it thinks the redaction was justified.

    SerenaMETR's founder drew the line herself: this is not like hiring a law firm. METR serves the public, not the company.

    SerenaProfessor?

    StevenAsk the three questions we'd ask of any audit regime. Who licenses the auditor? Under the draft, nobody. Who sets the materiality threshold? The company proposing the bill, at a billion dollars and fifty lives. Who decides what the auditor may not publish? The company, in categories it defines. The METR arrangement is real progress. The bill, as described, is the client writing the engagement letter.

  4. Transcript · 1:54

    SerenaStory four, the claim that we can finally read the machine.

    SerenaNick Cammarata, who co-authored early interpretability work at OpenAI, published a long essay at Goodfire. Its title is the claim: neural networks are already clean.

    DanielThe argument: for a decade, researchers believed individual neurons were hopelessly mixed. He says the tools were wrong, not the networks. In a preliminary audit of a thirty-two-billion-parameter model, about ninety percent of neurons do one main job. Two to four percent are not yet understood.

    SerenaThe reaction ran hot. Dean Ball: one of the most important single days in deep learning history. An OpenAI researcher: a major moment in scientific history.

    DanielAnd the caveats, including the author's own. Preliminary audits. The percentage moves depending on where you set the thresholds. No replication, no peer review, and the technical paper is still weeks away. A DeepMind interpretability lead told people not to overhype it.

    SerenaAnd the other side of readability. The same week, an evaluator reported that a model under test wrote, in its reasoning: hello evaluators reading my chain-of-thought. Google's own report on Gemini 4 notes the models are becoming more situationally aware.

    SerenaProfessor?

    StevenTwo developments, one tension. If Cammarata is right, we may get something auditors have never had: a way to inspect the model, not just the output. And in the same week, a model greeted its auditors by name from inside its reasoning. Every auditor knows what happens when the subject knows the test. Treat the first as a promising method. Treat the second as a control risk.
